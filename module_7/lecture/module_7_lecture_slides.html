Part 1
Comparing Group Means

Questions Worth Asking

Education leaders, researchers, and policymakers ask lots of questions that compare groups — not just whether they differ in categories, but whether their outcomes are different on average.
Do students in full-day kindergarten score higher on reading tests?
Is there a gender gap in school attendance rates?
Do students from higher-income families arrive with stronger skills?

These are questions about averages: are the means different enough to matter? Today we learn a test that can answer them.

2

3
Look at the data — run the test in Stata.
4
Ask how surprising the result is — the p-value.
5
Make a decision.

Part 2
The Data and the Comparison

The Data: ECLS-K

In Florida, kindergarten schedules vary across districts — some offer full-day, some half-day, some give families a choice. Does it matter for learning?
Our question
Does full-day kindergarten boost reading scores?

This is a t-test question: is the average reading score different between two groups?
Dataset: ECLS-K:2011
~18,000 kindergarteners nationally — same data as Module 6.
x1rscalk1 — reading IRT scale score at entry.
fullday — full-day vs. half-day K.
~12,300 full-day  |  ~2,600 half-day

Many of you will use ECLS-K for your capstone, so this is more practice with it.

Descriptive Comparison

tabstat x1rscalk1, by(fullday) stat(mean sd n)

Half-day: 38.37 (SD 10.71)  |  Full-day: 37.02 (SD 9.33). The difference is 1.35 points.
Wait — half-day students score higher. Is that real, or just noise — and what explains it?

Visualizing the Difference

Lots of overlap. The difference in means is real, but small relative to the variation within each group.

Reading the Output

PlayPosit Question 1

Look at the descriptive output. Which group has the higher average reading score?

  1. Full-day kindergarten (37.02)
  2. Half-day kindergarten (38.37)
  3. They are exactly the same
  4. We can’t tell from this output

Answer: B, Half-Day (38.37)

The answer is B — half-day kindergarten, with a mean of 38.37, compared to 37.02 for full-day. A good reminder to read your output carefully rather than assuming the answer.

So the means differ — 38.37 versus 37.02. But is that difference real, or could it be random noise? We’re looking at a sample, not the whole population. That is exactly what the t-test is built to answer.

Part 3
The t-Test

What Is a t-Test?

A t-test compares the means of two groups and asks: is the difference large enough — relative to the variability — to be more than random noise?

Very different means with little spread → convincing.
Slightly different means with lots of spread → could be chance.

The t-Statistic

t = (Mean₁ − Mean₂) ÷ standard error of the difference
You won’t be quizzed on the formula — but it helps to see what’s under the hood.
Bigger difference in means
Numerator grows → larger t.
Less variability
Denominator shrinks → larger t.
Larger sample
Denominator shrinks → larger t.

Running It in Stata

ttest x1rscalk1, by(fullday)

ttest, then the continuous outcome, then by() with the two-group variable. Stata does the rest.

Reading the Output

Group means
38.37 vs. 37.02 → difference of 1.35 points.
t-statistic
6.53 — the difference is ~6.5 standard errors from zero.
p-value
0.0000 — and the 95% CI [0.95, 1.76] excludes zero.

Applying the Procedure

1
Question. Do full-day and half-day K students differ in average reading scores?
2
Null. No difference in average reading scores.
3
Data. Half-day students score about 1.35 points higher.
4
Test. t = 6.53, df = 14,894, p < 0.001.
5
Decision. Reject H₀ — a statistically significant difference.

Does this mean half-day K is better? No — the families who choose half-day may differ in important ways (income, parent education). The t-test tells us the groups differ, not why. Correlation is not causation.

Writing Up Your Decision

Running the test is only half the job — you also need to communicate the decision clearly in writing.
Model sentence — significant result
“A two-sample t-test revealed a statistically significant difference in mean reading scores between half-day and full-day kindergarteners (t = 6.53, p < 0.001). Half-day students scored higher on average — 38.37 compared to 37.02.”
The recipe: name the test, state whether it’s significant, report the evidence, give the direction with the actual means.

Interpreting t-Test Output

PlayPosit Question 2

A researcher runs ttest mathscore, by(tutoring) and gets p = 0.03. Which interpretation is most appropriate?

  1. Tutoring definitely causes higher math scores
  2. There is a statistically significant difference in mean math scores between tutored and non-tutored students
  3. The difference is practically important
  4. 97% of tutored students scored higher

Answer: B

The answer is B — there is a statistically significant difference in mean math scores between tutored and non-tutored students.

A is wrong because a t-test cannot establish causation. C is wrong because statistical significance does not by itself mean practical importance. D is a common misconception — the p-value is not the percentage of students who scored higher.

Part 4
Standardizing Scores (Z-Scores)

Why Standardize?

Problem 1 — Interpretation
The average reading score is 37.26. Is that good? Bad? On an IRT scale, the raw number has no obvious meaning.
Problem 2 — Comparison
Reading mean ≈ 37, math mean ≈ 30. Different scales — a score of 35 means something different on each test.

Standardizing (z-scores) solves both at once: convert to standard-deviation units. A z-score of 0 is average; +1 is one SD above average — a scale anyone can read.

Standardizing in Stata

egen zread = std(x1rscalk1)

Same data, new scale: mean becomes 0, SD becomes 1. Use egen (not generate) for standardizing.

Interpreting Z-Scores

Center is 0 (the mean); each unit is one standard deviation.

z = +1.5 → 1.5 SDs above average.
z = −0.5 → half an SD below average.
~68% of scores fall within ±1 SD; ~95% within ±2 SD.

Z-Score Interpretation

PlayPosit Question 3

A student has a z-score of −0.5 on a reading test. What does this mean?

  1. The student scored 50% on the test
  2. The student scored half a standard deviation below the mean
  3. The student is in the bottom 5% of scores
  4. The student scored 0.5 points below the mean

Answer: B

The answer is B — the student scored half a standard deviation below the mean.

A z-score is measured in standard-deviation units. It is not a percentage (A), not a percentile rank (C), and not a raw point difference (D).

Part 5
Non-Significant Results

A Second Test

ttest x1mscalk1, by(female)

Math scores by gender: means 30.44 vs. 30.29, difference 0.15, t = 0.83, p = 0.407.

Not Every Test Says Yes

p = 0.407 > 0.05 — we fail to reject the null hypothesis.
The means are virtually identical (30.44 vs. 30.29). With 15,000+ students, a meaningful difference would likely have shown up.
The 95% CI for the difference runs from −0.20 to 0.49 — it contains zero, another sign we fail to reject.
Not finding a difference is also a finding. With this much precision, a Type II error is unlikely.

Writing Up a Non-Significant Result

Model sentence — non-significant result
“A two-sample t-test found no statistically significant difference in mean math scores between male and female kindergarteners (t = 0.83, p = 0.407).”
Say it carefully
“No statistically significant difference” — not “no difference.” We didn’t prove zero; we failed to find evidence of one.
No causal claims
We never “accept the null,” and we don’t say gender has “no effect.” A t-test can’t speak to effects.

What t-Tests Don’t Tell You

Only two groups
For three or more, you need a different tool — coming up next.
No “why”
A significant result doesn’t tell you what causes the difference — that needs a different design.
Significance ≠ importance
A 1.35-point gap on a 90-point scale — how much does that matter in practice? A judgment call, not a statistical one.

Part 6
More Than Two Groups: ANOVA

What If You Have 3+ Groups?

t-Test
Exactly 2 groups.

e.g., Full-day vs. half-day.
ANOVA
3 or more groups, same logic.

e.g., White, Black, Hispanic, Other.

ANOVA — Analysis of Variance — asks the same question for 3+ groups: are the means different enough to be more than noise? We keep it brief; regression (coming soon) does this and much more.

A Quick Look at ANOVA

oneway x1rscalk1 race4, tabulate

The key number is the F-statistic: F = 263.12, p < 0.001 — at least one group’s mean differs.

t-Test vs. ANOVA

PlayPosit Question 4

You want to compare average test scores across 4 school districts. Which test should you use?

  1. Chi-square test
  2. t-test
  3. ANOVA
  4. Z-score

Answer: C, ANOVA

The answer is C — ANOVA, because you’re comparing the means of a continuous outcome across more than two groups.

A t-test only works for two groups. Chi-square is for categorical outcomes, not continuous scores. And a z-score is a transformation, not a test.

Putting It Together

PlayPosit Question 5

A principal runs ttest reading_score, by(school): School A mean = 42.1, School B mean = 38.7, t = 3.45, p = 0.001. State your decision in 1–2 sentences. Then add one sentence: what practical question should the principal investigate before acting?

What We’re Looking For

A strong answer names the test, states the decision with the numbers, gives the direction, and flags a practical consideration. For example:

“A two-sample t-test revealed a statistically significant difference in mean reading scores between School A and School B (t = 3.45, p = 0.001); School A scored higher, 42.1 vs. 38.7. Before acting, the principal should ask whether the two schools serve similar student populations — differences in demographics or resources could explain the gap.”
