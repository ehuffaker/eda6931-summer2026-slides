Part 1 of 4
Conditional Means

Unconditional vs. Conditional

A comparison is just two or more conditional means placed side by side.
Unconditional mean
The average computed over all observations in the data. “What is the average reading score across all districts?”
Conditional mean
The average for one group: you put a condition on who is included. “What is the average reading score among urban districts?”
Everyone
80
split by group →
Boys
78
Girls
82
One overall average splits into a mean for each group, and those side-by-side group means are the comparison.

Conditional Means in Stata

Add if to restrict a command to a subgroup.

summarize meanread if urban == 1
summarize meanread if urban == 0

Watch the double equals. A condition uses == (“is it equal to?”). A single = is used later in the course to create a variable. Mixing them up is a common error, so it is worth slowing down on.

All Groups at Once

Two faster tools than writing one if line per group.
bysort
Runs the same command separately for each group.

bysort urban: summarize meanread
tabstat
Puts the comparison in one clean table.

tabstat meanread, by(urban) statistics(n mean sd)
In our data, urban districts average 310.7 and non-urban 308.3, a gap of about 2.4 scale-score points.

PlayPosit Question 1

A principal calculates the average daily attendance rate for Title I schools only. What kind of statistic is this?

  1. An unconditional mean: it uses every school
  2. Both, depending on how it is reported
  3. A conditional mean: it is restricted to one group
  4. Neither: attendance cannot be averaged

Answer: C, Conditional Mean

The average is restricted to one group, Title I schools, so a condition is placed on which schools are included. That makes it a conditional mean.

The unconditional version would be the average attendance rate across all schools, with no group restriction. In Stata, the conditional version is summarize attendance if titlei == 1.

Part 2 of 4
Comparing Across Groups and Over Time
Grades are one example of an ordered series; the same logic covers years, semesters, or any sequence you compare across.

Comparing Across Groups

Two examples with tabstat. Read the whole table, not just the averages.
tabstat meanread, by(locale) statistics(n mean sd) 
  locale |   N    Mean     SD
---------+-------------------
    City |  21   311.1   10.4
   Rural |  57   308.5   11.2
Suburban |  78   310.7    9.9
    Town |  45   308.1    9.7
---------+-------------------
   Total | 201   309.5   10.3
Read down the SD column: Rural’s spread (11.2) is the widest, so rural districts vary the most.
tabstat meanread, by(high_poverty) statistics(n mean sd) 
  high_poverty |   N    Mean     SD
---------------+-------------------
 Lower Poverty |  99   311.7   10.0
Higher Poverty | 102   307.4   10.2
Compare the Mean column: a 4.3-point gap by poverty.

Comparing Across Grades

The same idea extends to any ordered series, such as years, semesters, or grade levels: a conditional mean for each point in the series.
collapse (mean) meanread, by(grade)
twoway connected meanread grade

Here, across grades: average reading scores rise from about 298 at grade 3 to 311 at grade 4 to 320 at grade 5. Scale scores are built to be vertically comparable, so this upward climb is expected: students learn more content each year.

When there are only a few grades, a tabstat table is enough. With many periods, a line graph communicates the trend better.

PlayPosit Question 2

A tabstat table shows that suburban districts have a higher mean teacher salary but a much larger standard deviation than rural districts. What does the larger standard deviation tell you?

  1. Suburban salaries are higher on average
  2. Suburban salaries are more spread out: more variation around the mean
  3. Suburban districts have more teachers
  4. The suburban mean is more accurate

Answer: B, More Spread

The standard deviation measures spread: how far values fall from their mean. A larger SD means suburban salaries vary more widely; some are well above the average and some well below.

It says nothing about the number of teachers (that is N), and the mean already tells us the average. Two groups can share the same mean and still differ sharply in spread, which is exactly why we report the SD alongside it.

Part 3 of 4
Visualizing Comparisons

Bar Graphs of Group Means

Bar graphs are the most familiar way to show group means.
graph bar (mean) meanread, over(locale)

By convention the y-axis starts at zero.

Because reading scores sit in the high 200s and 300s, the bars look almost identical: the differences are real but small relative to the score scale. That flatness is honest information, and it is also a reason a box plot or line graph sometimes communicates more.

Box Plots Show the Whole Distribution

A box plot shows more than the mean.
graph box meanread, over(grade)

The line in the box is the median; the box is the middle 50% (25th to 75th percentile); the whiskers reach the extremes; dots are outliers.

Across grades the boxes step upward and barely overlap: a clear difference. How much the boxes overlap is the visual cue for how distinct the groups really are. Next, the same plot by locale.

Box Plot: Reading by Locale

The same box plot, now split by locale.
graph box meanread, over(locale)

Same command, just over(locale). Compare these boxes with the grade box plot: look at how much they overlap and how close the medians sit.

PlayPosit Question 3

In the box plot of reading by locale, the four boxes overlap heavily and their medians sit close together. What does that suggest?

  1. City districts clearly outperform the others
  2. Reading scores differ sharply across locale types
  3. A box plot cannot compare more than two groups
  4. Locale types are fairly similar in reading; the differences are small

Answer: D, Locale Types Are Similar

Heavy overlap between the boxes means the locale groups are not very distinct on reading: knowing a district’s locale tells you little about its score. The gap in the means (308 to 311) is small relative to the spread within each group.

Contrast that with the grade box plot, where the boxes barely overlapped and the difference was clear. Overlap is the visual cue for how different groups are, and here it says “not much.”

Part 4 of 4
Comparing One District to Another

Gatorville Is One District Among Many

This week’s dataset places Gatorville County Schools inside the Florida district data, as one more district alongside the other 66.
What’s in the file
Every Florida district at grades 3 to 5, plus Gatorville, with one row per district per grade. Same variables for all: meanread, lvl3plus, locale, poverty, and more.
Why it’s set up this way
Because every district is in one file, you can line up Gatorville against any other district using the if condition you already know. No merging required.

How Does Gatorville Compare?

Gatorville sits a little below Alachua County at every grade.
sort grade
twoway (connected meanread grade if districtname==“GATORVILLE”)
      (connected meanread grade if districtname==“ALACHUA”)

Grade 3: 294 vs. 298. Grade 4: 306 vs. 312. Grade 5: 318 vs. 321. Two lines, each just a conditional if plot. Sort by grade first so the line connects in order.

A leader seeing this would look first at grade 4, the widest gap.

PlayPosit Question 4

Gatorville’s grade-4 average (306) is about 6 points below Alachua’s (312). Based on this comparison alone, what can we conclude?

  1. That Gatorville is behind Alachua at grade 4, but not yet whether the gap is large enough to be meaningful
  2. That something in Gatorville is causing lower scores
  3. That the gap is too small to matter
  4. That Gatorville’s grade-4 teachers are less effective

Answer: A

The comparison tells us Gatorville is behind Alachua at grade 4. It does not tell us why (that would be a causal claim, B and D), and it does not tell us whether six points is a meaningful gap or one we might see by chance (C jumps to a conclusion).

Describing the difference is exactly what we can do now. Judging whether it is large enough to take seriously is the work of statistical inference, the focus of Module 5.

Stretch: Comparing to the State Average

Optional stretch. Not required, and it uses a new command. A push for anyone who wants to go further.

To compare Gatorville against all districts at once, build the state average for each grade with egen, then plot it against Gatorville:

bysort grade: egen state_avg = mean(meanread)

The story is the same as the Alachua comparison: Gatorville sits a little below the statewide average at every grade.
