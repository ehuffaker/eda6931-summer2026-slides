The Problem

“Our data is just one snapshot.”

Every dataset contains randomness. Scores fluctuate. Districts vary. If we collected the data again next year, or looked at a different set of districts, the numbers would come out a little differently.

Statistical inference is the toolkit that helps us decide when a pattern in data is likely real and when it might just be noise.

Part 1
Populations and Samples

Population vs. Sample

Our Florida data: 66 districts, one snapshot.

The question: what can this snapshot tell us about the bigger picture?

We never observe the whole population directly. All we actually see is a sample, and that sample could have come out differently.

Probability: The Language of Uncertainty

Probability puts a number on “how likely is something?”

10 coin flips → 6 heads? Not surprising.
10 coin flips → 10 heads? Very surprising (less than 0.1% chance).

Same logic with our data: how likely is a 2.3-point difference by chance alone? The lower that probability, the more confident we can be that the difference is real.

The Normal Distribution

Most values cluster near the middle. Extreme values are rare.
FL reading scores: mean ≈ 312, SD ≈ 10.5 → most districts fall between ~302 and ~323.

How Unusual Is Unusual?

We can calculate what percentage of values fall above or below any point.
This lets us quantify how surprising a result is.

Population vs. Sample

PlayPosit Question 1

In our Module 4 analysis, we compared reading scores for 66 Florida districts. If we wanted to generalize our findings to all U.S. school districts, what role does our Florida data play?

  1. It is the population
  2. It is a sample
  3. It is neither: you can’t generalize from Florida to the whole country
  4. It depends on the research question

Answer: B, A Sample

The 66 Florida districts are a sample: a subset of the larger group we want to learn about, which here is all U.S. districts.

Whether Florida is a good sample for generalizing to the whole country is a separate, important question. But the core idea is that we use a part to learn about a whole. Notice the answer hinges on which population we have in mind: if we only cared about Florida districts, these 66 would be much closer to the full population.

Part 2
The Central Limit Theorem

A Thought Experiment

Imagine drawing 100 different random samples of Florida districts.

Each sample gives a slightly different average, but they cluster around the true value.
This is the Law of Large Numbers.

The Central Limit Theorem

Even if individual data points are messy, the distribution of sample averages is approximately normal.
This is why the bell curve keeps showing up.

The Measuring Stick

Is our observed result bigger than what random chance would produce?
Middle of the distribution → not surprising. Out in the tail → surprising.

The Central Limit Theorem

PlayPosit Question 2

According to the Central Limit Theorem, if we repeatedly drew random samples of school districts and calculated the average reading score each time, those sample averages would:

  1. Be identical every time
  2. Be completely unpredictable
  3. Form an approximately normal (bell-shaped) distribution centered on the true average
  4. Get farther and farther from the true average

Answer: C, A Bell Curve

The sample averages form an approximately normal distribution centered on the true average. That is the key insight of the CLT.

Even if individual data points are scattered, the averages from repeated samples form a predictable bell curve. That predictability is exactly what makes statistical inference possible: we can estimate how much “bounce” to expect from random chance alone, and judge whether our result is bigger than that.

Part 3
Hypothesis Testing

The Null Hypothesis

Null Hypothesis (H₀)
“There is no real difference.” The assumption we test against.
Alternative Hypothesis (H₁)
“There IS a real difference.” What we suspect may be true.

Think of it like a courtroom: we assume “not guilty” (no effect) until the evidence convinces us otherwise.

H₀ for our example: “There is no real difference in reading scores between urban and non-urban districts. The 2.3 points is just random noise.”

The P-Value

The probability of seeing a result this extreme (or more extreme) if the null hypothesis were true.

Small p-value (e.g., 0.02): surprising. The null hypothesis is not believable.

Large p-value (e.g., 0.40): not surprising. Consistent with no real difference.

Statistical Significance

If p < 0.05, we call the result “statistically significant.”

This threshold is a convention, not a law of nature. A p-value of 0.049 and a p-value of 0.051 are practically identical, but one gets called “significant” and the other doesn’t.

The Procedure

1
Start with a question. Is there a real difference between urban and non-urban districts?
2
Assume no difference. The null hypothesis: any gap is just random noise.
3
Look at the data. We found a 2.3-point gap in reading scores.
4
Ask: how surprising is this? Calculate the p-value, the probability of this result under the null.
5
Decide. Is the p-value small enough to reject the null hypothesis?
Next week you’ll do each of these steps in Stata.

Interpreting a P-Value

PlayPosit Question 3

A researcher compares test scores between two groups and reports p = 0.03. What does this mean?

  1. There is a 3% chance that the groups are actually the same
  2. The treatment caused a 3% improvement
  3. If there were truly no difference between the groups, there would be only a 3% chance of observing a difference this large or larger
  4. 97% of students improved

Answer: C

p = 0.03 means: if there were truly no difference between the groups, there would be only a 3% chance of observing a difference this large or larger.

This is one of the most commonly misunderstood ideas in statistics. The p-value is not the probability that the null hypothesis is true (answer A). It is the probability of seeing data this extreme if the null were true. That distinction is exactly what the Nuzzo article you’re reading this week is about.

Part 4
Errors and Confidence Intervals

Two Ways to Be Wrong

False Positive (Type I)
A district adopts an expensive new reading program because the evaluation showed “significant” improvement, but in reality the program had no effect. Resources wasted on a false alarm.
False Negative (Type II)
A promising tutoring program actually works, but the evaluation, with a small sample, fails to detect the effect. The district cuts a program that was helping students.
No test eliminates uncertainty. It just quantifies it, and there’s a tradeoff between the two errors.

Confidence Intervals

Instead of just “significant or not?” a confidence interval gives a range of plausible values.

Urban–Non-Urban difference: 2.3 pts, 95% CI [−0.2, 4.8]. The interval includes zero, so we can’t rule out no real difference.
“Not significant” ≠ “no effect.” It means we don’t have enough evidence to be sure.

Type I and Type II Errors

PlayPosit Question 4

A school district concludes that a new reading program significantly improved student achievement, but in reality the program had no effect. What type of error did the district make?

  1. Type I error (false positive)
  2. Type II error (false negative)
  3. No error: the statistics showed significance
  4. A measurement error

Answer: A, Type I

This is a Type I error, a false positive, a false alarm. The district concluded there was a real effect when there wasn’t one.

When we use a significance threshold of p < 0.05, we are accepting a 5% chance of making exactly this kind of mistake. This is why replication and practical significance matter alongside statistical significance: a single “significant” result can still be a false alarm.

Part 5
Practical Significance

A Superintendent’s Dilemma

Scenario. A superintendent sees that School A outperforms School B by 5 points on the state reading assessment. With 10,000 students in each school, the difference is statistically significant (p = 0.001).

Is 5 points enough to justify overhauling the curriculum?

With large samples, even tiny differences become statistically significant. Whether to act requires professional judgment, not just a p-value.

Two Lenses

Statistical Significance
“Is the difference likely real?” Could chance explain it?
Practical Significance
“Is the difference big enough to matter?” Should we act on it?
Education leaders need both lenses.

Practical Significance: Compared to What?

A 5-point difference sounds like a number. But is it a lot? To answer, you need context.
Compared to the scale
Is 5 points big on this test? On a 500-point scale it’s 1%. On a 50-point scale it’s 10%.
Compared to the spread
If the standard deviation is 30 points, a 5-point gap is modest. If it’s 8 points, that’s substantial.
Compared to the cost
The program costs $2,000 per student. Is a 5-point gain worth that, or could those resources do more elsewhere?
Compared to other interventions
Do similar programs produce bigger gains? Smaller? We need benchmarks to calibrate expectations.

Statistical vs. Practical Significance

PlayPosit Question 5

A new after-school tutoring program raised reading scores 2 points over non-participants, “statistically significant (p < 0.01).” Should the superintendent expand it district-wide? What else would help her decide?

Respond in 2–3 sentences.

What We’re Looking For

There’s no single right answer, but a strong response recognizes that statistical significance alone doesn’t tell her whether to act. Good responses raise questions like:

  • Is a 2-point gain practically meaningful for students (effect size relative to the scale and the spread)?
  • What does the program cost, and could those resources do more elsewhere?
  • Was the study design credible? How was the comparison group chosen?
  • Would the result hold for different student populations?

The takeaway: leaders need both whether a difference is real and whether it is large enough to justify action.

Part 6
Review

The Full Reasoning Chain

1
We observe a difference in our data (2.3 points).
2
But our data is just a sample. It could have come out differently.
3
The CLT tells us how much averages should vary by chance.
4
We ask: is our observed difference surprisingly large?
5
The p-value quantifies “how surprising.” The CI gives a plausible range.
6
Then we ask: even if it’s real, is it big enough to act on?
