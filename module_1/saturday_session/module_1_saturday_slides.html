What can we not learn from quantitative data?

Quantitative data are often, at their core, the result of counting. That counting gives us useful information about what is or isn’t happening in schools, but it definitely doesn’t tell us everything.

What quantitative data can tell us

  • The scale of an issue across a district (one school at 27%, another at 11%)
  • Patterns across schools, grades, years
  • How GCS compares against the district average, and against the state
  • Where to look more closely

What quantitative data cannot tell us

  • Why any particular student is absent
  • How to support a specific student or family
  • The mechanisms behind a pattern
  • What the numbers leave out
If we only rely on what we see in our interactions on a day-to-day basis, we can miss the scale of certain phenomena and schools. If we only rely on these quantitative measures, we can miss the story and an explanation of the mechanisms underlying larger patterns.
Part 2 · Course Overview

Frame this as the natural companion to the warm-up. The figure tells us something important (which schools, what scale) and is silent on something equally important (why, who, what to do). Without counting, chronic absenteeism stays anecdotal and we underestimate how widespread it is. Without conversation, we have rates with no understanding. The course teaches the counting half of that pairing. Module 9 will return to this directly when we read others’ research.

The Quantitative Education Data Landscape

Sources and access

Quick transition slide. State out loud that we are moving from course orientation into the data landscape segment, what data we work with as researchers and administrators and where it comes from.

What are the types of quantitative data that are typically used in education?

Public

No special permission needed.

  • Aggregate data at school, district, and state level (FL DOE, NCES Common Core of Data, IPEDS, NAEP)
  • De-identified individual records (ECLS-K public-use file, NLSY, ACS microdata)
  • Aggregator and compiler sites that package federal data (Urban Institute Education Data Explorer, Data Lumos at UMich, Stanford SEDA)
  • State-released public-use data with student-level records (Massachusetts is a leader; some other states are catching up)

Restricted-use

Individual-level, some identifiable. Partnership and approval required for research.

  • District administrative data: enrollment, attendance, grades, assessments, discipline. Most relevant for many of you.
  • State longitudinal data systems (Florida K-20 Data Warehouse, Texas ERC, Wisconsin ERS)
  • Federal restricted-use files (full ECLS-K, full NLSY, IPEDS restricted)
  • Vendor and third-party data (EdTech platform logs, assessment vendors, partnership-only datasets)
Both are great sources for dissertation projects. The next few slides cover the FERPA, DSA, and IRB pieces that come with restricted-use access.

FERPA: the legal floor for student data

FERPA (Family Educational Rights and Privacy Act) governs what schools can disclose about student records. The default rule is consent. The interesting parts are the exceptions.

Four exceptions worth knowing

  • School officials with legitimate educational interest: internal staff doing their jobs.
  • Studies and research: disclosure to organizations conducting studies for the school, under a written agreement, without consent.
  • Directory information: name, address, dates of attendance can be released unless families opt out.
  • Audit and evaluation: federal and state agencies evaluating programs.
FERPA is the legal floor, not the ceiling. State laws often go further (Florida’s K-20 Education Code is one example), and district policies often go further still. When in doubt, ask your district’s data steward.

Data access procedures

Research
Application
From researcher to data holder
Proposes research questions, methods, the data needed, and the value of the work. The first step in any research-data partnership, whether with a district, a state repository (e.g., Texas ERC), or a federal agency.
DSA / DUA
Data Sharing / Use Agreement
Contract between data holder and recipient. What is shared, for what purpose, under what storage and destruction rules. The two acronyms are often used interchangeably.
IRB
Institutional Review Board
Human-subjects research approval. Required for research with student data, even when de-identified. Re-identification risk persists, and ethics review is required regardless.
You may see both sides of these procedures: requesting data as an internal or external researcher, or deciding as the administrator whether and how to share student data for research.

Looking ahead to your dissertation

The clearest path to approval is when researcher and district leadership are aligned on a question. Ask if your superintendent or other school leaders have particular challenges or questions that could be answered using district data. If you are interested in using administrative data for your dissertation, start building that relationship now.

Two pieces of paperwork in practice. DSA and DUA are functionally the same contract under different names: one specifies what’s shared, the other governs what happens after, often combined. IRB is the human-subjects layer, required even for de-identified data because re-identification risk is real. The “studies and research” FERPA exception ties to all of this.

Our solution for this course: simulated district data

For this course we use simulated data from Gatorville County Schools (GCS), a fictional Florida district designed to look like the real thing.

Calibrated to match Florida district averages on demographics, attendance, and achievement. However, there are no real students and no privacy concerns.

  • 10 elementary schools, ~4,500 student-year records across 3 years
  • Demographics, attendance, test scores, and a teacher climate survey
  • Calibrated to Florida grade-3 benchmarks so the patterns feel real

What we’re doing in Stata today

We are not answering any formal research questions today. We are just going to get familiar with the mechanics of working in Stata.

Why are we doing this?

Statistical software like Stata lets us perform analyses and document them in a way that is replicable. That is a key feature of academic research, distinct from, for example, generating figures in Excel. It leaves a paper trail.

  1. Open UFApps and launch Stata
  2. Upload the GCS data file to your UFApps drive
  3. Open the data file in Stata
  4. View the data: variables, types, a few rows
  5. Run a few simple commands (describe, summarize, tabulate, histogram)
  6. Take a screenshot of your output (we will learn to save figures properly in future assignments; today, screenshots are fine)
  7. Download your work from UFApps to your local computer

There will be several Stata assignments throughout the semester with many opportunities to practice. Working with Stata over UFApps can be frustrating; my goal today is to give us plenty of time to troubleshoot together.
