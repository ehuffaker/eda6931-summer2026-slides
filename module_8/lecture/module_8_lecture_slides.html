Part 1
From t-Tests to Regression

A New Kind of Question

Every test so far compared groups: chi-square asked whether categories were related; the t-test asked whether two means differed. This week the question changes: how much does one variable change when another changes?

As temperature goes up, sales go up — and the line says by how much per degree.
Correlation tells you how strong the relationship is. Regression tells you how much Y changes when X moves.

Questions Worth Asking

The kinds of questions correlation and regression can answer:
Does family income predict kindergarten reading scores?
How strong is that relationship — and how much of the variation does it explain?
Can we draw a single line that summarizes thousands of data points?

We are no longer asking whether groups differ. We are asking how much Y changes when X changes.

Seeing the Relationship

The Data: ECLS-K

Our question
Does SES predict kindergarten reading scores?

Reading Scores and SES

scatter x1rscalk1 x12sesl

Every dot is one student. The cloud drifts upward — but with a lot of spread at every SES level.
How do we put a number on how strong this relationship is?

What Is Correlation?

The correlation coefficient r measures the strength and direction of a linear relationship. It ranges from −1 to +1: sign = direction, magnitude = strength.

Caveat: r only captures linear relationships — a U-shape would give r ≈ 0 despite a real pattern.

Correlation in Stata

pwcorr x1rscalk1 x12sesl, sig

r = 0.40, p < 0.001 — a moderate positive correlation. Rule of thumb: below 0.3 weak, 0.3–0.7 moderate, above 0.7 strong.
We know the relationship exists. Can we describe it more precisely — with a line?

Interpreting r

PlayPosit Question 1

A researcher finds that the correlation between family income and kindergarten reading scores is r = 0.40, p < 0.001. Which of the following is the best interpretation?

  1. Higher family income causes higher reading scores.
  2. There is a moderate positive linear relationship between family income and reading scores.
  3. 40% of the variation in reading scores is explained by family income.
  4. The relationship is weak because r is less than 0.50.

Answer: B, a Moderate Positive Relationship

The answer is B — a moderate positive linear relationship. Correlation tells us direction and strength.

It does not tell us that one variable causes the other (A). And r is not the percentage of variation explained (C) — that’s R², which we’ll get to shortly. An r of 0.40 is conventionally moderate, not weak (D).

Part 3
The Line of Best Fit

The Line of Best Fit

scatter x1rscalk1 x12sesl || lfit x1rscalk1 x12sesl

Correlation gave us how strong (r = 0.40). The line gives us how much: a formula for predicting scores.
Among all possible lines, this one does the best job summarizing the relationship.

Why This Line? (OLS Intuition)

Each vertical segment is a residual: observed minus predicted. OLS (Ordinary Least Squares) finds the line that makes the squared residuals, added up, as small as possible.
The concept matters here, not the formula — minimize the overall prediction error.

Running a Regression in Stata

regress x1rscalk1 x12sesl

A lot of output — but only four numbers really matter. Let’s break them down.

Reading the Output

Slope (x12sesl) = 4.842
For each 1-unit increase in SES, reading scores increase by ~4.84 points, on average.
Intercept (_cons) = 37.840
Predicted reading score when SES = 0.
R² = 0.1613
SES explains about 16% of the variation in reading scores.
p-value = 0.000
Statistically significant — essentially impossible under “no relationship.”

Interpreting the Coefficient

PlayPosit Question 2

A regression of reading scores on SES produces a slope of 4.84 with p < 0.001. What does the 4.84 mean?

  1. The average reading score in the sample is 4.84.
  2. For each 1-unit increase in SES, reading scores increase by 4.84 points, on average.
  3. 4.84% of the variation in reading scores is explained by SES.
  4. Students with high SES score 4.84 points higher than students with low SES.

Answer: B, the Rate of Change

The answer is B — for each 1-unit increase in SES, reading scores increase by 4.84 points on average. That’s the core interpretation of a regression coefficient: it tells you the rate of change.

It’s not the sample mean (A), not R² (C), and not a two-group comparison (D) — that would be a t-test, and “high” vs. “low” isn’t defined here.

Part 4
Interpreting the Results

What the Slope Means

The SES index spans about 5 units (−2.3 to +2.6). Across that full range, the predicted difference is about 5 × 4.84 ≈ 24 points — a substantial predicted gap between the lowest- and highest-SES students.
Model sentence for your write-up
“A one-unit increase in SES is associated with a 4.84-point increase in reading scores (p < 0.001, 95% CI [4.66, 5.02]).”

“Associated with” — not “causes.” We’ll come back to that.

What R² Tells You

SES alone explains 16% of the variation — meaningful for a single predictor of a complex outcome.
The other 84%: school quality, family structure, motivation, prior learning, and more.

A low R² does not mean the relationship is unimportant. It means other factors also matter — which is almost always true in social science.

The Five-Step Procedure (Adapted)

1
Start with a question — does SES predict reading scores?
2
Assume no relationship — H₀: the slope = 0.
3
Look at the data — run the regression in Stata.
4
Ask how surprising the result is — the p-value.
5
Make a decision.
Same logic as the t-test — applied to a regression.

Applying the Procedure

1
Question. Does SES predict kindergarten reading scores?
2
Null. The slope = 0 — no linear relationship.
3
Data. Slope = 4.84, SE = 0.09, t = 52.05.
4
Test. p < 0.001 — extremely unlikely under H₀.
5
Decision. Reject H₀ — a statistically significant positive relationship.

The coefficient is significantly different from zero: higher SES is associated with higher reading scores.

Writing Up Your Results

A model paragraph you can adapt for your capstone:
“A bivariate regression was conducted to examine the relationship between SES and kindergarten reading scores (N = 14,088). Results indicate that SES is a significant predictor of reading scores (slope = 4.84, SE = 0.09, p < 0.001). For each one-unit increase in SES, reading scores increase by approximately 4.84 points. SES explains about 16% of the variation in reading scores (R² = 0.16).”
Sample size, slope with SE and p, the plain-language rate of change, and R² — every element in one paragraph.

R² and Practical Importance

PlayPosit Question 3

The regression of reading scores on SES has R² = 0.16. A colleague says: “Only 16% — SES barely matters.” How would you respond?

  1. They’re right — an R² below 0.50 means the relationship is unimportant.
  2. R² tells us how much variance is explained, but 16% can still represent a meaningful and policy-relevant relationship — especially when a single variable explains it.
  3. R² = 0.16 means the regression is not statistically significant.
  4. You can’t interpret R² without knowing the sample size.

Answer: B, 16% Is Still Meaningful

The answer is B. An R² of 0.16 means SES explains 16% of the variation — meaningful for a single predictor of a complex outcome.

There is no universal cutoff for “important” (A). Significance is assessed by the p-value, not R² (C). And R² stands on its own as a descriptive measure of explanatory power (D).

Comparing R² Across Models

PlayPosit Question 4

Two regressions both predict scores from SES. The reading model has R² = 0.16 and the math model has R² = 0.19. What can you conclude?

  1. SES is a significant predictor of math but not reading.
  2. SES explains a slightly larger share of the variation in math scores than in reading scores.
  3. The math regression is more accurate because 0.19 is closer to 1.0.
  4. The difference (0.03) is too small to be meaningful.

Answer: B, a Slightly Larger Share for Math

The answer is B — SES explains a slightly larger share of the variation in math scores (0.19) than in reading scores (0.16).

Both models are statistically significant, so A is wrong. Neither model has a high R², so “more accurate because closer to 1.0” overstates it (C). And B is the straightforward factual conclusion the numbers support (D).

Part 6
What Regression Doesn’t Tell You

Correlation ≠ Causation

Ice cream sales and drowning rates are correlated — but ice cream doesn’t cause drowning. Both rise in summer.
Schools with more computers have higher test scores — but buying computers doesn’t necessarily raise scores. Wealthier schools buy more computers and have students with other advantages.
Higher SES predicts higher reading scores — but giving families money may not raise scores. SES is bundled with books at home, parent education, school access.

Our regression shows an association. It does not prove that changing SES would change scores. Module 9 digs deeper into why.

Other Limitations

One predictor at a time
School quality, parenting, motivation — none of it is controlled for. Module 10 adds control variables.
Sensitive to outliers
A few extreme values can pull the line. Always look at your scatter plot before interpreting the numbers.
Assumes linearity
Regression fits a straight line. If the real relationship is curved, the line may mislead.
Keep these in mind when interpreting your results.

The Superintendent’s Claim

PlayPosit Question 5 · Reflection

A superintendent shows a regression where districts that spend more per pupil have higher test scores. She concludes: “If we increase spending, scores will go up.” What questions would you ask about this claim? What might the regression be missing?

Respond in 2–3 sentences.

What We’re Looking For

The regression shows a correlation between spending and scores — it doesn’t prove that spending causes higher scores. Wealthier districts may spend more and have students with other advantages: higher family income, more educated parents, better facilities.

Without controlling for those factors, we can’t conclude that increasing spending alone would raise scores. A strong answer names at least one confounding factor and recognizes that a bivariate regression controls for nothing else.
