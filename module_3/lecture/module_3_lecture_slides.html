What Is Research?

What Is Research?

Research is the systematic process by which we develop new knowledge from evidence.
Exploration (Modules 1-2)
Open a dataset. Run summarize. Make a histogram. Ask: what does this variable look like? The goal is familiarity with the data.
Research (Module 3 onward)
Start with a specific question. Choose an analytic strategy that can answer it. Draw conclusions supported by evidence. The goal is new knowledge.

Quantitative Research Is Influential in Education

“We review the research on the different programs, products, practices, and policies in education. Then, by focusing on the results from high-quality research, we try to answer the question ‘What works in education?’”
— Institute of Education Sciences, What Works Clearinghouse
Federal and state education policy relies on quantitative evidence to guide decisions about programs, funding, and accountability. The Every Student Succeeds Act (ESSA) requires states to use “evidence-based” interventions. The evidence base behind those standards is largely quantitative.

Two Types of Research Questions

Two Types of Research Questions

Descriptive
What is happening? Provide descriptive statistics for an outcome (e.g., test score trends over time). Identify relationships between a predictor (e.g., attendance) and an outcome (e.g., student grades).
Focus of this course.
Causal
Does X cause Y? Demonstrate a predictor’s causal impact on an outcome (e.g., to what extent does attendance determine grades?). Aims to remove “selection bias” from the descriptive relationships we observe between variables.
Covered conceptually later in the course, but we will not conduct causal analyses.

Research Question Examples

Descriptive

• What are attendance patterns across elementary schools in a district?

• How do teacher retention rates vary by school locale (urban, suburban, rural)?

• How have patterns of school segregation changed in the past decade?

Causal

• Does participation in a high-dosage tutoring program improve students’ math achievement?

• What is the effect of reducing class size on teacher job satisfaction?

• Does sports participation influence student attendance?

Notice the language: descriptive questions ask what, how, and how much. Causal questions use words like improve, effect, and influence — they claim one thing produces a change in another.

PlayPosit Question 1

A district administrator asks: “How has the four-year graduation rate in our district changed over the past decade?” Is this a descriptive or causal research question?

  1. Descriptive — it documents a trend over time
  2. Causal — it asks whether something caused the graduation rate to change
  3. Both — it is descriptive but implies a causal claim
  4. Neither — it is not a research question

Answer: A — Descriptive

The question asks what happened to graduation rates over time. It documents a trend. It does not ask why the rate changed or whether a specific policy or program caused the change.

If the administrator asked “Did our new advising program increase the graduation rate?” — that would be a causal question, because it claims a specific intervention produced a specific outcome.

Test Score Data

Test Score Data: What the Numbers Represent

In Module 4, you will use the Gatorville data to compare test scores across groups of students and benchmark those comparisons against Florida statewide data. Before you start, it helps to understand what test scores actually represent and why we use scale scores rather than proficiency rates for analysis.
Proficiency Rate
The percentage of students scoring at or above a predetermined cut score. Simple and intuitive: “62% of third graders met the standard.” Used for accountability (school grades, ESSA), identifying students for intervention, and comparing subgroups.
Limitation: the cut score is a policy choice, not a natural boundary. Move it up or down and the rate changes — same students, same learning.
Scale Score
A continuous number on a standardized metric that preserves the full distribution of student performance. A given score means the same thing across test forms, grades, and years. Mean, standard deviation, and sub-scores are all available.
In the GCS assessments data, scale_score is a scale score for each student-subject-year. In the Florida DOE data, meanread is an average reading scale score at the district-grade level.

Why Scale Scores Are Preferred for Analysis

Proficiency rates are useful for communication — “65% met the standard” is easy for a school board to interpret. But for analysis, scale scores give you the full picture.
Proficiency rate
“62% met the standard.” One number. Information about variation within and across groups is lost. Two schools with the same rate may have very different underlying distributions.
Scale score
Mean = 312, SD = 24. The full distribution is preserved. You can compute meaningful averages, make precise comparisons, and detect differences that proficiency rates would hide.
The type of score you use changes the story you tell. For this course, you will work with scale scores because they preserve the information you need to make rigorous comparisons.

The construct-measure gap from Module 2 applies here: test scores are a measure of academic achievement, not the construct itself.

PlayPosit Question 2

A researcher wants to compare average reading achievement across 66 Florida school districts. Should they use scale scores or proficiency levels for this comparison, and why?

  1. Scale scores — they are continuous and preserve variation across districts
  2. Proficiency levels — they are easier to interpret and communicate
  3. Either would work equally well for this purpose
  4. Neither — district-level comparisons require a different measure

Answer: A — Scale Scores

For comparing averages across districts, scale scores are the right tool. They are continuous, preserve the full range of variation, and allow you to compute meaningful means and make precise comparisons.

Proficiency levels would tell you the percentage of students at each level, which is a different (and also useful) comparison — but it does not give you an average achievement measure.

This is exactly the kind of analysis you will do in Module 4 with the Gatorville and Florida statewide data.
