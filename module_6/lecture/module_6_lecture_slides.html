Part 1
Comparing Groups

Questions Worth Asking

As education leaders, you often want to know whether group membership matters for outcomes. These questions sit at the heart of school-choice debates like vouchers.
Are children from non-English-speaking homes less likely to attend private school?
Is race/ethnicity associated with who has access to private schooling?
Does having a college-educated parent predict private school attendance?

These aren’t questions about averages — they’re about whether categories line up in ways that are more than coincidence. Today we learn a statistical test that can answer them.

The Procedure (Revisited)

1
Start with a question.
2
Assume no difference — the null hypothesis.
3
Look at the data.
4
Ask: how surprising is this? — the p-value.
5
Make a decision.
Last time, we learned the logic. Today, we fill in every step with real data.

Part 2
Cross-Tabulation

The Data: ECLS-K

The Early Childhood Longitudinal Study (ECLS-K:2011). A shift from the Florida DOE aggregate data we’ve used so far to student-level survey micro-data.
What is it?
A nationally representative study of ~18,000 children surveyed at kindergarten entry. Each observation is one child — demographics, family background, school type, and test scores.
Key variables today
private — attends a private school?
nonengl — home language other than English?
female, race6, momed — gender, race/ethnicity, mother’s education.

Many of you have said you’ll use ECLS-K for your final project, so this week we’ll get some more practice with it. What we’re asking: who has access to private schooling?

Cross-Tabulation

tab nonengl private

English homes: 14.4% private  |  Non-English homes: 6.7% — half the rate.
Is this gap real, or just random variation?

Reading the Cross-Tab

PlayPosit Question 1

Look at the cross-tabulation. Among children from non-English-speaking homes, how many attend private school?

  1. 2,400
  2. 172
  3. 1,925
  4. 2,572

Answer: B, 172

The answer is 172 — the count in the non-English row, private-school column.

The other numbers are nearby totals: 2,400 is non-English children in public school, 2,572 is the non-English row total, and 1,925 is the total number of private-school children. Reading a cross-tab means finding the cell where the right row and the right column meet.

Part 3
Expected Frequencies

What If There’s No Relationship?

Expected = (Row total × Column total) ÷ Grand total
Under “no relationship,” we’d expect 335 non-English families in private school. We observed only 172 — about half.

Expected vs. Observed

The bigger the gaps between observed and expected, the more evidence against “no relationship.”

Expected Frequencies

PlayPosit Question 2

If there’s no relationship between two categorical variables, expected frequencies are calculated based on…

  1. Equal counts in every cell
  2. The row and column totals
  3. Zero
  4. The overall mean

Answer: B, Row and Column Totals

Expected frequencies come from the row and column totals: Expected = (Row total × Column total) ÷ Grand total.

The logic is “fair share.” If about 13% of all children attend private school, then under no relationship we’d expect about 13% of each language group in private school — not equal counts in every cell, and not zero. Expected frequencies describe what the table would look like if the null hypothesis were true.

Part 4
The Chi-Square Test

The Chi-Square Statistic

A single number that summarizes how far ALL the cells are from what we’d expect if there were no relationship.

Small chi-square → table looks close to what chance would produce.
Large chi-square → big deviations → evidence of a relationship.

Stata calculates this for you — no formula needed.

Running It in Stata

The chi2 option tells Stata to run the chi-square test after the cross-tabulation.

Reading the Output

p < 0.001: if there were truly no relationship between home language and school type, this result would be extremely unlikely.

Applying the Procedure

1
Question. Is home language related to private school attendance?
2
Null hypothesis. No relationship — any pattern is random variation.
3
Data. Non-English families attend private school at about half the expected rate.
4
Test. Chi-square = 110.59, p < 0.001.
5
Decision. Reject H₀ — strong evidence of a relationship.

Module 5 reminder: even with p < 0.001, a false positive (Type I error) is possible — but very unlikely here.

Interpreting Chi-Square Output

PlayPosit Question 3

A researcher runs tab gender major, chi2 and gets Pr = 0.23. Which conclusion is most appropriate?

  1. Gender and major are definitely unrelated
  2. Gender causes students to choose certain majors
  3. There is not enough evidence to conclude that gender and major are related
  4. The researcher made a Type I error

Answer: C

With p = 0.23 — well above 0.05 — the right conclusion is there is not enough evidence to conclude that gender and major are related.

Notice what we do not say. We don’t say they’re “definitely unrelated” (A) — failing to find evidence isn’t proof of no relationship; that would risk a Type II error. We don’t claim causation (B). And a non-significant result is not a Type I error (D) — a Type I error is a false positive, concluding there’s a relationship when there isn’t.

Part 5
Creating Variables in Stata

When Can We Use Chi-Square?

1
Both variables must be categorical. Not continuous — chi-square works with counts in categories.
2
Expected frequency ≥ 5 in each cell. Check with tab var1 var2, chi2 expected.
3
Observations must be independent. Each child appears only once in the table.
Our ECLS-K data meets all three. Always check before interpreting your own results.

Why Create New Variables?

Raw data rarely comes in the exact form you need. Creating new variables is one of the most common data-preparation tasks.
Some tests need categories
Chi-square requires categorical variables — you may need to convert continuous data into groups.
Collapse to prevent small cells
Too many categories can mean cells with very few observations, making tests unreliable.
Make results interpretable
“College degree vs. not” is clearer than an 8-category education variable.
Match your research question
“Do children of college-educated mothers attend private school at different rates?” needs a binary college variable.
This is a skill you’ll use in every analysis, not just chi-square.

Creating a Binary Variable

generate college = (momed >= 6) if momed != .

Creates 1 if the condition is true, 0 if false. Stata treats missing (.) as larger than any number, so the if momed != . guard stops missing values from being counted.

Creating Variables

PlayPosit Question 4

Which Stata command correctly creates a binary variable that equals 1 if the mother has a college degree (momed is 6 or higher)?

  1. create college if momed >= 6
  2. generate college = (momed >= 6) if momed != .
  3. recode momed college 6
  4. label college = momed >= 6

Answer: B

The correct command is generate college = (momed >= 6) if momed != .

generate is the command that creates a new variable; the parentheses make a true/false condition that becomes 1 or 0; and if momed != . keeps missing values missing. The others use commands that don’t work this way: create isn’t a Stata command for this, recode has different syntax, and label attaches text to values — it doesn’t build a variable.

Part 6
Interpreting the Results

A Second Test

About 13% of both boys and girls attend private school. Nearly identical rates, and the test is not significant.

What Chi-Square Doesn’t Tell You

No direction
It says there’s a relationship, not which way it goes. You read the table to interpret the pattern.
No “which cells”
In a larger table, a significant result doesn’t tell you which specific cells are driving it.
Significance ≠ importance
With a large sample, even a trivial relationship can be statistically significant. Always ask: does this matter in practice?
Chi-square is a starting point. Interpreting the pattern, and deciding whether to act, takes professional judgment.

Significance and Importance

PlayPosit Question 5

A state agency reports a statistically significant chi-square test showing that school locale (urban, suburban, town, rural) is associated with whether districts meet state proficiency targets. What does this tell you? What doesn’t it tell you? What would you want to know next?

Respond in 2–3 sentences.

What We’re Looking For

There’s no single right answer, but a strong response separates what the test establishes from what it doesn’t:

  • What it tells you: locale and meeting targets are associated — the pattern is unlikely to be random chance.
  • What it doesn’t: not which locales do better (no direction), not which categories drive it, and not whether the gap is large enough to matter.
  • What’s next: look at the cross-tab itself, consider what’s behind locale (poverty, funding, staffing), and ask whether the difference is practically meaningful.
